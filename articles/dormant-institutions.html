Institutions & Accountability

Dormant Institutions

Rana Mohsin Ali · 25 October 2026 · First published on LinkedIn

In my previous article, Fading Academia, I questioned whether the expansion of architectural education in Pakistan has kept pace with the skills, technological expertise, and professional maturity demanded by the industry.

But the problem does not end at graduation. It raises a more fundamental question: when education fails to prepare professionals adequately, are our regulatory and professional institutions ensuring that the built environment still meets society's needs?

Pakistan has established institutions, accreditation frameworks, professional bodies, and building regulations. PCATP's own accreditation criteria recognize the importance of design competence, technical skills, critical thinking, and professional ethics (PCATP Accreditation Guide). The Institute of Architects Pakistan also identifies professional development, research, and public interest among its objectives (IAP).

The challenge, therefore, is not simply the absence of frameworks; it is whether these frameworks consistently translate into measurable outcomes.

Are building regulations being effectively enforced? Are architectural standards evolving with climate change, resource scarcity, digital engineering, artificial intelligence, and changing social needs? Are universities, regulators, professional bodies, researchers, and the construction industry working together to prepare for the challenges ahead?

Internationally, the direction is clear. The revised 2026 UNESCO–UIA Charter for Architectural Education addresses ecological disruption, social equity, professional ethics, and responsible AI integration. These priorities demonstrate why architectural development cannot remain confined to conventional curricula, drawing approvals, or compliance checklists.

Pakistan needs a stronger connection between research and practice, regular review of building standards, transparent enforcement, continuous professional development, and post-occupancy evaluation of completed buildings. Universities should help test future solutions; regulators should translate evidence into enforceable standards; professional bodies should promote competence and accountability; and public authorities should measure whether buildings actually serve their users.

This is not an argument that every Pakistani architect or institution has failed. Nor is it a call to copy foreign models without considering local realities. It is a call to evaluate the system by what it delivers, rather than what it claims to represent.

If Fading Academia questions the quality of professionals we are producing, institutional accountability questions the quality of the future we are building.

The ultimate measure of success is not the number of architecture schools, registered professionals, regulations, conferences, or approved drawings. It is whether our buildings and cities become safer, more inclusive, more sustainable, more resilient, and better prepared for generations to come.

Pakistan has the talent and institutional foundations to make progress. What is needed is stronger coordination, evidence-based reform, and accountability for outcomes.

Are our institutions merely administering the present, or actively preparing Pakistan's built environment for the future?

Right of reply. This piece names PCATP and the Institute of Architects Pakistan as frameworks, not as failures.
