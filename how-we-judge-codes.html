How we judge codes

Code Fix is our series on Pakistan's building codes and standards. Each episode takes one code, or one chapter of a code, and asks two questions. Is the text precise? Is it actually applied? This page sets out the method we use to answer them, so anyone can check our reasoning.

Every judgement on this page is applied to codes and systems, never to individuals. Our verdicts are our professional judgement on the cited evidence, and they are labelled as opinion. We propose; the bodies that hold the authority decide.

Step one: the code today

We start by stating what the code is: its title, edition, legal instrument (for example, its SRO), the international standard it is based on and that standard's age, and who it binds. Every one of these facts is verified under our Standards.

Step two: six precision tests

We test the code, clause by clause where it matters, against the current edition of the international standard it is based on.

  1. Currency. Is the text based on a current edition of its international basis, and is it on a review cycle that keeps it current?
  2. Clarity. Is each requirement measurable? A clause that sets a number, a test method or a performance class can be checked. A clause that says "adequate" or "suitable" cannot.
  3. Completeness. Does the code cover the topics a current code of its kind covers, or are whole subjects left out?
  4. Local fit. Do the requirements suit Pakistani conditions: climate, hazards, building stock, materials, products, skills and laboratories?
  5. Internal consistency. Does the code agree with itself, and with the other codes, bylaws and laws that apply to the same building?
  6. Enforceability. Is the code notified, adopted into the bylaws that permits are issued under, and checked by someone with the authority and the capacity to do so?

Step three: the verdict

Each code receives one of four verdicts.

  1. Precise and current. Measurable, and based on a current international edition.
  2. Adequate but dated. Measurable, but one or more editions behind its international basis.
  3. Imprecise or obsolete. Vague, internally conflicting, or based on a withdrawn standard.
  4. Missing. No Pakistani instrument covers the subject. We use this verdict only when an official source confirms it, such as an authority's published list of codes or a gazette index.

Step four: content gap or implementation gap

The six tests sort every problem into one of two kinds. We name each gap with one type, because each type needs a different fix.

A content gap is a problem in the text itself. The text is out of date, vague, incomplete, unfit for local conditions, or in conflict with another instrument. The fix is a revised text.

Illustrative example: a fire clause requires "adequate means of escape" but sets no maximum travel distance to an exit. A designer cannot show compliance, and a checker cannot test it. That is a content gap under the clarity test.

An implementation gap is a problem in how the text reaches buildings. The text may be sound, but it is not notified, not adopted into the bylaws, not checked at plan approval or on site, not enforced, or not reviewed on schedule. The fix is a procedure, not a new text.

Illustrative example: a current, measurable code is published, but the city's building bylaws do not refer to it. Plans are approved under the bylaws alone, so the code's requirements are never checked. That is an implementation gap under the enforceability test.

Both examples are illustrations of the method, not findings about any particular Pakistani code. Our findings on specific codes appear in each episode, with their sources.

Step five: the evidence

Each finding rests on a clause-level comparison with the current international standard, named by edition and clause, and on documented consequences where they exist. Every point meets our verification rule: the primary document, or two independent sources. Where a finding names a gap, we also show how the best-performing countries handle the same issue, with sources, so the fix has a working model.

Step six: how a proposed revision is drafted

  1. Labelled as a discussion draft. Every revision is marked "Archiverse discussion draft, not an official code". It is a proposal for discussion. We never present it as law or as settled policy.
  2. Adapted, never copied. Each revised requirement is written in our own plain language and cites the international clause it adapts, by standard, edition and clause. We do not reproduce copyrighted standard text. For an implementation gap, the draft is a procedure instead: who adopts the code, who checks it, when, and how the results are published.
  3. Qualified review. Each draft goes to qualified volunteer reviewers in the relevant fields, such as structural, fire, building services and law. Any engineering value in a draft needs a qualified reviewer's sign-off before it is published. Reviewers are named only with their consent.
  4. Thirty days of public consultation. The draft is published for public comment for 30 days. We invite PEC and the relevant authorities to share their position.
  5. "You said / we changed." After the consultation we publish a table of what people said and what we changed in response, including the points we did not adopt and why.
  6. Formal submission. The final proposal goes formally to PEC, the provincial governments and the relevant authorities. Their replies are published and tracked.
How to read a Code Fix episode. Facts about a code are verified facts with sources. The verdict, the gap type and the proposed revision are our opinion, offered for discussion. Comments and proposals from readers are filed in our comment library and can shape the next draft.
